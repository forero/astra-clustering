\author{ASTRA clustering project}

\begin{abstract}
Using the complete grid of full-box runs on disk (nine AbacusSummit
cosmologies $\times$ 50 HOD draws), we ask which ASTRA environment-split
statistic, or which pair of statistics, extracts the most cosmological
Fisher information beyond the plain full-sample auto-correlation.  We sweep
all 16 single quantile vectors and all 120 pairs, in two framings
(standalone and added to the full auto), scoring each by the
HOD-marginalised Fisher figure of merit.  The naive ranking is dominated by
vectors containing a \emph{random}-quantile leg, with apparent gains up to
$55\times$ (all of the global top~20 carry a random leg).  We trace this not to
the randoms being noise --- their environment-classified \emph{monopoles} carry
real cosmic-web signal --- but to their \emph{quadrupoles}, which have no
peculiar-velocity (RSD) signal and whose noisy derivatives inflate the Fisher;
flagging the random legs is thus a conservative choice pending derivative-noise
propagation.  Among the clean \emph{data}-leg vectors the gains are
real: adding a single full$\times$data-quantile cross buys $\sim\!5\times$ in
the three-parameter FoM, and a greedy forward selection that spans the four
density quantiles reaches a five-stem vector
(\code{full}$+$\code{xdQ1}$+$\code{dQ1}$+$\code{dQ2}$+$\code{xdQ3}$+$\code{dQ4})
with $\FoM_3 = 43\times$ --- marginalised errors tighter than the standard 2PCF
by $3.4\times$ ($\omega_b$), $4.1\times$ ($\omega_c$), $3.6\times$ ($n_s$) and
$2.8\times$ ($\sigma_8$), with clear diminishing returns past four stems.  We
document the method, the random-quadrupole diagnosis, the honest ranking, and the greedy
chain.
\end{abstract}

\section{Question and data}

ASTRA splits a galaxy sample into four environment quantiles
(Q1 underdense $\to$ Q4 overdense) and measures, per box, the
multipoles $\xi_{0,2}(s)$ of: the full-sample auto-correlation
(\code{tpcf\_full\_data}, our baseline), the four data-quantile autos
(\code{dQ1..4}), the four random-quantile autos (\code{rQ1..4}), the four
full$\times$data-quantile crosses (\code{xdQ1..4}), and the four
full$\times$random-quantile crosses (\code{xrQ1..4}).  That is
$17$ stems $\times\{\ell=0,2\}\times 15$ bins per run.

The signal data are the $9\times 50 = 450$ phase-matched full-box runs
(\code{data/fullbox/}); cosmology derivatives come from the global linear
response fit (\code{derivative\_global\_*}, condition number $1.34$ on the
full grid).  The covariance of one $500\Mpch$ subbox is estimated from the
mean-subtracted subboxes pooled across all nine cosmologies,
$9\times 64 = 576$ fluctuation samples, and scaled to the full
$2000\Mpch$ box volume ($C/64$).  The HOD nuisance covariance $C_{\rm HOD}$
is the empirical scatter of the 50 same-phase \code{c000} draws.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

The analysis is implemented in \code{scripts/fisher\_vector\_search.py},
which reuses the assembly and Fisher machinery of
\code{scripts/fisher\_joint.py}.  The steps are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Candidate vectors.}  Each candidate is one or two ASTRA stems,
  taken at \emph{native} 15-bin resolution in mono$+$quad ($\ell=0,2$).  The
  576-sample pooled covariance keeps the Hartlap factor benign even at
  $n_b=60$ (pair) or $90$ (pair added to the full auto), so no rebinning is
  needed.  We evaluate the 16 singles and all $\binom{16}{2}=120$ pairs.
\item \textbf{Two framings.}  \emph{Standalone} scores the candidate by
  itself against the full auto (``is it better than the 2PCF?'').
  \emph{Additive} scores the full auto with the candidate appended
  (``what does ASTRA add to the 2PCF?'').  The additive framing is the
  physical measure of marginal information and is our headline.
\item \textbf{Fisher and marginalisation.}  For each candidate we build the
  $16\times16$ joint Fisher over the four cosmological parameters
  $\{\omega_b,\omega_c,n_s,\sigma_8\}$ and the twelve yuan23 HOD parameters,
  $F = D\,C^{-1}D^{\mathsf T}$ with Hartlap-corrected precision, add a
  Gaussian yuan23 prior on the HOD block, and read off the
  HOD-marginalised $4\times4$ cosmology covariance
  $\mathrm{Cov} = (F+F_{\rm prior})^{-1}_{[\rm cosmo]}$ (route a).
\item \textbf{Figure of merit.}  We reduce $\mathrm{Cov}$ to
  \begin{equation}
    \FoM_4 = \big[\det \mathrm{Cov}_{4}\big]^{-1/2}, \qquad
    \FoM_3 = \big[\det \mathrm{Cov}_{3}\big]^{-1/2},
  \end{equation}
  where $\mathrm{Cov}_3$ drops $\sigma_8$ (the upper-left $3\times3$ block,
  already marginalised over $\sigma_8$).  The \emph{gain} of a candidate is
  $\FoM/\FoM_{\rm full\,auto}$.  We headline $\FoM_3$ because the $\sigma_8$
  derivative is unreliable (Sec.~\ref{sec:caveat}), so a four-parameter FoM
  inherits that unreliability.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background: how the covariance and the derivatives are estimated}
\label{sec:background}

This section explains, in simple terms, the two main ingredients of the Fisher
matrix in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fisher}: the covariance $C$ (how noisy the measurement is)
and the derivatives $D$ (how the measurement changes when a parameter changes).
It is written for a reader who is new to the method.

\subsection*{The simulation boxes}

All our data come from the AbacusSummit simulations.  Each simulation is a cubic
box with a side of $2000\Mpch$ (about $3$ billion light-years).  Galaxies are
placed inside the box with an HOD (halo occupation distribution) model, at
redshift $z=0.5$.  We use nine different cosmologies.  For each cosmology we have
$50$ galaxy catalogues, each one with different HOD parameters.  So we have
$9\times50 = 450$ boxes in total.  All boxes use the same random ``phase''
(called \code{ph000}): they start from the same initial pattern of matter.  This
matters because, when we compare two boxes, the random differences between them
(``cosmic variance'') then mostly cancel.

\subsection*{The covariance, from subboxes}

The covariance tells us how much our measurement would change if we measured it
again in a different patch of the Universe.  Ideally we would use many separate
big boxes, but big boxes are expensive to simulate.  Instead we cut one full box
into smaller cubes.  We divide the $2000\Mpch$ box into $4\times4\times4 = 64$
subboxes, each with a side of $500\Mpch$.  We measure the data vector in every
subbox.  The scatter among the $64$ subboxes gives an estimate of the covariance
of one $500\Mpch$ subbox.

One box gives only $64$ subboxes.  That is not many compared with the length of
our data vector (up to $180$ numbers).  To do better we combine the subboxes from
all nine cosmologies.  For each cosmology we first subtract its own mean, so that
only the random fluctuations remain, and then we put all the subboxes together.
This gives $9\times64 = 576$ samples.  Here we assume the level of noise is about
the same in every cosmology, which is a good approximation over our small range
of cosmologies.

Our real measurement uses the full $2000\Mpch$ box, not a small subbox.  A larger
volume gives a smaller error.  The variance goes down in proportion to the volume,
so we divide the subbox covariance by $64$ (the number of subboxes in a full box)
to get the covariance of the full box.

Finally, a covariance estimated from a limited number of samples gives a slightly
biased result when we invert it.  We correct this with the standard ``Hartlap
factor'', $(N-n_b-2)/(N-1)$, where $N=576$ is the number of samples and $n_b$ is
the number of data points.  For the five-stem vector ($n_b=180$) this factor is
$0.685$.  Using $576$ samples instead of $64$ is exactly what lets us keep the
quadrupole at full resolution: with only $64$ samples a $180$-point vector could
not be inverted at all (we would need $N>n_b$).

\subsection*{How robust is this covariance?}
\label{sec:covrobust}

It is fair to ask how much we can trust a covariance built this way.  We checked
it in three ways, and the short answer is: it is robust enough for
\emph{comparing} data vectors (the main goal of this note), but the
\emph{absolute} error bars should be read with some caution.

The reassuring test is a ``jackknife'': we rebuild the covariance nine times,
each time leaving one cosmology out of the pool ($8\times64 = 512$ samples), and
recompute the five-stem $\FoM_3$.  The result changes by only about $6\%$ (about
$2\%$ per parameter).  So the answer does not depend on which cosmologies we pool,
and because every data vector uses the \emph{same} covariance, their ranking is
trustworthy.

There are, however, three weaknesses that the jackknife does not see:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The subboxes are not fully independent.}  The $64$ subboxes come
  from a single box, so they share the largest waves and neighbouring subboxes
  are correlated.  The true number of independent samples is therefore below
  $576$.  This means the real Hartlap correction should be a little stronger than
  the $0.685$ we use, and the ratio of samples to data points (here $576/180
  \approx 3.2$) is in practice even smaller.  A ratio of only a few is modest:
  the inverse covariance is somewhat noisy, and indeed the correlation matrix has
  a large condition number ($\sim\!10^4$), meaning some directions are close to
  unconstrained.
\item \textbf{Open boundaries.}  A $500\Mpch$ subbox has open edges and feels
  large-scale ``super-sample'' modes; the full $2000\Mpch$ box is periodic and
  does not.  Dividing the subbox covariance by $64$ assumes a simple volume
  scaling and ignores this difference, so the absolute covariance, especially on
  large scales, is only approximate.
\item \textbf{Pooling assumption.}  Pooling across cosmologies assumes the noise
  is the same for every cosmology.  This is mild over our small parameter range,
  but it is an assumption.
\end{itemize}

In short, the \emph{relative} results (which vector wins, by how much) are robust
to roughly the $6\%$ level; the \emph{absolute} $\sigma$ values should be treated
as estimates good to perhaps $10$--$20\%$.  The clean way to remove these
approximations is to estimate the covariance from many \emph{independent} full
boxes --- for example the $25$ AbacusSummit phases (\code{ph000}--\code{ph024})
measured as periodic boxes, or a large suite of approximate mock catalogues ---
which would give many more than $576$ truly independent samples and drop the
open-boundary problem entirely.  That is the recommended next step before
quoting absolute error bars.

\subsection*{The derivatives, from a response model}

The Fisher matrix also needs the derivatives: how the data vector changes when we
change each parameter by a small amount.  We get these from a ``global response
model''.  We fit one simple straight-line (linear) relation to all $450$ boxes at
the same time, separately for each bin of the data vector:
\begin{equation}
\xi \;=\; \xi_0 \;+\; \sum_p a_p\,\theta_{{\rm cosmo},p}
                \;+\; \sum_q b_q\,\theta_{{\rm HOD},q},
\end{equation}
where the $\theta_{\rm cosmo}$ are the four cosmological parameters and the
$\theta_{\rm HOD}$ are the twelve HOD parameters.  The slopes $a_p$ are the
cosmology derivatives; the slopes $b_q$ are the HOD derivatives.  Because the HOD
terms are fitted at the same time, the cosmology slopes come out ``clean'': they
are not spoiled by the fact that different cosmologies happen to have different
HOD values.  And because all boxes share the same phase, cosmic variance cancels
in the slopes.  This fit is very well behaved on the full grid (its condition
number is $1.34$).

\subsection*{A more flexible fit: the Gaussian Process}

The straight-line fit assumes the data vector changes in exact proportion to each
parameter.  The true response is only approximately linear.  To test this we also
used a Gaussian Process (GP).  A GP is a flexible method that learns a smooth
curve from data without assuming a straight line; as a bonus, it also gives an
error bar on every prediction.

We trained one GP for each cosmology.  The input was the twelve HOD parameters and
the output was the data vector, using that cosmology's $50$ catalogues as training
points.  The HOD derivative is then the slope of the GP curve at the central HOD
point.  Two things came out of this test.  First, the HOD response is mildly
nonlinear: the GP fits the data clearly better than the straight line (it captures
about $75\%$ of the variation, against about $18\%$ for the line).  Second, only a
few HOD parameters --- mainly the typical mass of the host halo and its scatter
--- drive most of the change; the other parameters matter little.

The headline numbers in this note use the simpler linear response model, because
it is stable and well understood on the full grid.  The GP is a refinement and a
cross-check; turning it into final derivatives that also carry their own error
bars is left for future work.  (The same GP idea, applied separately in each
cosmology and evaluated at one common HOD point, is also how we can build
``HOD-matched'' derivatives, but that is beyond the scope of this note.)

Table~\ref{tab:raw} and the bright \code{rQ4}/\code{xrQ4}/\code{xrQ1} band
in Fig.~\ref{fig:heatmap} show that the naive sweep is dominated by vectors
containing a random leg, with implausibly large gains ($\FoM_3$ up to
$55\times$).  Taken at face value this would say the random-quantile statistics
are the most powerful of all, which deserves scrutiny.  The key is to separate
the monopole from the quadrupole; the trap is the \emph{quadrupole}, not the
random statistic as a whole.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The random-quantile monopoles carry real signal.}  Once the
  uniform random points are classified by their Delaunay environment, the
  overdense quantile genuinely sits in overdense regions and the underdense one
  in voids.  Their auto-correlation therefore measures the clustering of the
  cosmic-web \emph{environments} themselves --- a real, cosmology-dependent
  quantity, not Poisson noise.  Indeed the extreme random monopoles are as
  signal-rich as the data quantiles (\code{rQ1},\code{rQ4} $\ell0$ noise/signal
  $\approx 0.05$--$0.09$).
\item \textbf{The random-quantile quadrupoles are physically weak.}  The
  quadrupole of the \emph{data} comes from peculiar velocities (redshift-space
  distortions).  The random points have \emph{no} peculiar velocities, so their
  quadrupole carries almost no real signal: the little anisotropy present is a
  geometric byproduct of the classification and is dominated by realisation
  noise (\code{rQ2/3} $\ell2$ noise/signal $\approx 0.7$--$0.8$).
\item \textbf{The inflation is derivative noise in the quadrupole.}  A Fisher
  matrix treats the derivative $D$ as exact; noise in $D$ that is not
  represented in the covariance $C$ inflates $D\,C^{-1}D^{\mathsf T}$, so the
  Fisher reads derivative noise as constraining power.  A mono$+$quad random
  vector carries a signal-rich monopole \emph{and} a noise-limited quadrupole,
  and it is the noisy quadrupole derivative that drives the spurious gains.  The
  signature fits: a random vector is barely better than the full auto on its own
  (\code{xrQ4} standalone $\FoM_3=1.6$) yet explodes when appended.
\end{itemize}

So the caution is narrower than ``the randoms are noise''.  Their monopoles are
genuine cosmic-web environment signal; it is the velocity-free quadrupoles, with
their noisy derivatives, that inflate the Fisher --- and, because the randoms
trace the \emph{same} density field as the data, large \emph{independent} gains
are in any case implausible on redundancy grounds.  Flagging every random-leg
candidate is therefore a \emph{conservative} choice, not a proof that they are
worthless: it removes the quadrupole-noise inflation at the price of also setting
aside the real random-monopole information.  The clean resolution is to propagate
the (emulator) derivative variance, which would deflate the noisy quadrupole
derivatives while keeping the genuine monopole signal (future work).  Until then
we base the physical conclusions on the data legs only.

\end{itemize}

\section{Greedy forward selection: how many stems, and which?}
\label{sec:greedy}

The single- and pair-level results raise the obvious question: if one extreme
data cross already buys $\sim\!5\times$ and the void auto$+$cross pair buys
$14\times$, how far does it go?  We answer it with a \emph{greedy forward
selection} restricted to the eight data-leg stems
(\code{scripts/fisher\_greedy\_chain.py}).  Starting from the full auto, at each
step we append the single data-leg stem that most increases $\FoM_3$, keep it,
and repeat, up to five added stems.  Greedy selection is not guaranteed to find
the globally optimal subset, but it is the natural way to read off the
\emph{marginal} value of each successive statistic and to expose redundancy.

The chain is \code{full}$+$\code{xdQ1}$+$\code{dQ1}$+$\code{dQ2}$+$\code{xdQ3}$+$\code{dQ4}
(Table~\ref{tab:greedy}, Fig.~\ref{fig:greedy}).  Three things stand out.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Strong diminishing returns.}  The per-step gain falls
  $5.1\to2.7\to1.8\to1.3\to1.3$.  The first two stems carry most of the
  information ($\FoM_3$ reaches $14$ of the eventual $43$ by step two); beyond
  step four each new statistic adds only $\sim\!30\%$, while the bin count and
  Hartlap/redundancy cost keep rising.  A four-stem vector is the practical
  sweet spot.
\item \textbf{Gains come from spanning environments, not repetition.}  Greedy
  does not pile up void statistics; after the void auto$+$cross it reaches for a
  \emph{different} density bin (\code{dQ2}), then mid--high (\code{xdQ3}), then
  the knot (\code{dQ4}), touching all four quantiles by step five.  Different
  environments respond to cosmology and to the HOD in different ways, so each new
  environment breaks a \emph{complementary} degeneracy; a second statistic from
  the same environment would be largely redundant.
\item \textbf{$\sigma_8$ moves only in combination.}  No single vector improved
  $\sigma_8$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}), but the cumulative five-stem vector
  tightens it $2.8\times$ ($1.4\times10^{-2}\!\to\!5.0\times10^{-3}$).  The
  multi-environment combination partially lifts the amplitude degeneracy that
  defeats any one statistic --- though $\sigma_8$ remains the least trustworthy
  entry because its derivative is unreliable, and $\FoM_3$ (which excludes it)
  stays our headline score.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{The five-stem vector versus the 2PCF}

Figure~\ref{fig:corner} is the head-to-head Fisher comparison of the standard
full-sample 2PCF (\code{tpcf\_full\_data}, mono$+$quad) and the five-stem ASTRA
vector, both HOD-marginalised, generated by
\code{scripts/fisher\_compare\_full\_vs\_5stem.py}.  The five-stem $68/95\%$
contours sit nested well inside the full-auto contours on every panel, with the
marginalised errors tighter by

\begin{equation}
\sigma(\omega_b)\ 3.4\times,\quad
\sigma(\omega_c)\ 4.1\times,\quad
\sigma(n_s)\ 3.6\times,\quad
\sigma(\sigma_8)\ 2.8\times,
\end{equation}

i.e.\ $\FoM_3 = 43\times$.  Crucially the blue ellipses are not merely smaller but
\emph{rotated} relative to grey: the environment splits change the orientation of
the parameter degeneracies, which is the geometric signature of genuinely new
information rather than a uniform rescaling.  The five-stem $95\%$ region is
inside the full-auto $68\%$ region for most parameter pairs --- a qualitatively
different constraint from a single $2\Mpch$ box, by reanalysis alone.

\section{Anatomy of the 5-stem Fisher computation}
\label{sec:anatomy}

This section opens up the Fisher calculation behind Fig.~\ref{fig:corner} step by
step, so every ingredient is visible.  The five-stem vector concatenates six
pieces (the full auto plus the five selected stems), each in mono$+$quad at the
native $15$-bin resolution, for a data vector of
$n_b = 6\times2\times15 = 180$ bins.  All figures are produced by
\code{scripts/fisher\_5stem\_details.py}.

The Fisher matrix is
\begin{equation}
F \;=\; D\,C^{-1}\,D^{\mathsf T},
\label{eq:fisher}
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the $(4+12)\times n_b$ matrix of derivatives of the data vector with
respect to the four cosmological and twelve HOD parameters, and $C$ is the data
covariance.  The cosmology constraints follow from inverting either the
cosmology block alone (conditional, HOD fixed) or the full matrix plus the HOD
prior and taking the cosmology sub-block (marginalised).  The four steps below
are the four factors in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fisher}.

\subsection*{Step 1 --- the data vector}

Figure~\ref{fig:step1} shows the fiducial (\code{c000} ensemble-mean) data vector,
one panel per piece.  The monopole amplitude grows monotonically from the void
(\code{dQ1}) to the knot (\code{dQ4}, an order of magnitude larger): the overdense
quantile is the most strongly clustered and highest-bias tracer, the underdense
one the least.  The quadrupoles ($\ell=2$) carry the redshift-space anisotropy.
These six curves, stacked $[\,\ell0,\ell2\,]$ per piece, are the 180-component
vector whose information content we are dissecting.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fisher_5stem/step1_data_vector.png}
\caption{Step 1: the fiducial five-stem data vector, $s^2\xi_{0,2}(s)$ per piece.
Amplitude grows void ($\protect\code{dQ1}$) $\to$ knot ($\protect\code{dQ4}$).}
\label{fig:step1}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Step 2 --- the derivatives $D$}

Figure~\ref{fig:step2} shows the four cosmology rows of $D$ (the global-response
derivatives $\partial\xi/\partial\theta$) across all 180 bins, with the six piece
blocks marked.  Two features matter for the Fisher information:
\begin{itemize}
\item The derivative amplitude is largest on the high-bias \code{dQ4} piece,
  especially at small $s$ --- the knot quantile is the most cosmology-responsive.
\item The \emph{shape} of the response differs from piece to piece (e.g.\ the
  $\omega_b$ acoustic feature versus the broadband $n_s$ tilt appear with
  different relative weights in each environment).  It is precisely this
  piece-to-piece difference in $\partial\xi/\partial\theta$ that lets the
  environment splits break parameter degeneracies the full auto cannot.
\end{itemize}
(The twelve HOD rows of $D$, not shown, enter the marginalisation in Step 4.)

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fisher_5stem/step2_derivatives.png}
\caption{Step 2: the four cosmology rows of $D$, $\partial\xi/\partial\theta$,
across the 180-bin vector.  Solid lines separate the six pieces; dotted lines mark
the $\ell0|\ell2$ split within each piece.  The high-bias \code{dQ4} piece carries
the largest response.}
\label{fig:step2}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Step 3 --- the covariance $C$}

Figure~\ref{fig:step3} is the $180\times180$ correlation matrix of the data
vector, estimated from the mean-subtracted subboxes pooled across all nine
cosmologies ($576$ samples) and scaled to the full-box volume.  The block
structure is the crux of how much \emph{independent} information the vector holds:
\begin{itemize}
\item Within each piece, neighbouring $s$-bins are positively correlated, and the
  monopole and quadrupole blocks are cross-correlated.
\item Across pieces, the full auto is strongly correlated with the
  full$\times$data crosses (\code{xdQ1},\code{xdQ3}) because they literally share
  the full-sample leg, and the quantile autos are mutually correlated through the
  shared density field.  These off-diagonal correlations are why the gains
  saturate: the pieces are not independent, and $C^{-1}$ correctly down-weights
  the redundancy.
\end{itemize}
The $576$ pooled samples are what make $n_b=180$ affordable: the Hartlap factor is
$(576-180-2)/(576-1)=0.685$, comfortably positive, where the $64$-subbox estimate
would have failed ($n_b>64$).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{fisher_5stem/step3_correlation.png}
\caption{Step 3: the $180\times180$ data-vector correlation matrix (pooled
576-subbox $C$).  Black lines separate the six pieces.  Strong cross-piece
correlations (e.g.\ full with the \code{xd} crosses) are the redundancy that
limits the cumulative gain.}
\label{fig:step3}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Step 4 --- the Fisher matrix and parameter covariance}

Combining $D$ and $C$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:fisher} with the Hartlap-corrected
precision gives the parameter covariance.  Figure~\ref{fig:step4} shows the
$4\times4$ cosmology parameter correlation matrices, conditional (HOD fixed) and
marginalised (HOD nuisances marginalised with the yuan23 prior), with the
per-parameter $\sigma$ in the titles:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
 & $\sigma(\omega_b)$ & $\sigma(\omega_c)$ & $\sigma(n_s)$ & $\sigma(\sigma_8)$ \\
\midrule
conditional (HOD fixed) & $2.9\times10^{-5}$ & $2.0\times10^{-4}$ & $7.6\times10^{-4}$ & $2.9\times10^{-3}$ \\
marginalised            & $1.7\times10^{-4}$ & $4.1\times10^{-4}$ & $3.6\times10^{-3}$ & $5.0\times10^{-3}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Two lessons:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Marginalising over the HOD costs a factor few} per parameter
  ($6\times$ on $\omega_b$, $5\times$ on $n_s$): the cosmology directions that
  overlap the HOD response are degraded most.  This is the honest price of not
  knowing the galaxy--halo connection, and it is the regime all our headline
  numbers are quoted in.
\item \textbf{Marginalisation re-shapes the degeneracies.}  Conditionally,
  $\omega_b$ is strongly anti-correlated with $\sigma_8$ ($-0.84$) and
  $\omega_c$ with $n_s$ ($-0.79$); after HOD marginalisation these soften and new
  correlations appear ($\omega_b$--$\omega_c=+0.55$, $n_s$--$\sigma_8=-0.42$).
  The rotation of the ellipses in Fig.~\ref{fig:corner} is exactly this change of
  correlation structure.
\end{itemize}

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fisher_5stem/step4_fisher.png}
\caption{Step 4: cosmology parameter correlation matrices from
$F=D\,C^{-1}D^{\mathsf T}$, conditional (left) and HOD-marginalised (right), with
the per-parameter $\sigma$ in the titles.  HOD marginalisation both inflates the
errors and re-shapes the degeneracies.}
\label{fig:step4}
\end{figure}

\section{Monopole, quadrupole, or both?}
\label{sec:multipole}

The vectors above use both multipoles; does the quadrupole earn its place?  We
test this two ways with \code{scripts/fisher\_multipole\_compare.py}: a
configuration comparison (mono-only, quad-only, mono$+$quad) for both the full
auto and the five-stem vector, and a greedy ``poll'' that treats each
(piece, multipole) as one of twelve selectable units and records whether the
monopole or quadrupole is chosen at each step (Fig.~\ref{fig:multipole}).

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Multipole configuration comparison, HOD-marginalised.  $\FoM_3$ gain is
relative to the standard 2PCF (full auto, mono$+$quad).  The quadrupole is weak
alone but adds substantially when combined, and is the main carrier of $\sigma_8$
information.}
\label{tab:multipole}
\begin{tabular}{llrrr}
\toprule
vector & config & $n_b$ & $\FoM_3$ gain & $\sigma(\sigma_8)$ \\
\midrule
full auto & mono ($\ell0$)  & 15 & 0.41 & $3.6\times10^{-2}$ \\
full auto & quad ($\ell2$)  & 15 & 0.12 & $4.0\times10^{-2}$ \\
full auto & mono$+$quad      & 30 & 1.00 & $1.4\times10^{-2}$ \\
\midrule
5-stem    & mono ($\ell0$)  & 90 & 12.1 & $1.2\times10^{-2}$ \\
5-stem    & quad ($\ell2$)  & 90 &  2.6 & $1.0\times10^{-2}$ \\
5-stem    & mono$+$quad      & 180 & 43.3 & $5.0\times10^{-3}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The monopole is the workhorse.}  On its own it carries most of the
  information --- $0.41$ of $1.0$ for the full auto, $12$ of $43$ for the five-stem
  vector.  The quadrupole alone is weak ($0.12$ and $2.6$).
\item \textbf{But the quadrupole is not redundant.}  Adding it to the monopole
  multiplies $\FoM_3$ by $2.4\times$ for the full auto ($0.41\to1.0$) and
  $3.6\times$ for the five-stem vector ($12\to43$).  Crucially it is the main
  source of $\sigma_8$ information: mono-only $\sigma(\sigma_8)$ improves
  $2.4\times$ when the quadrupole is added ($1.2\times10^{-2}\to5.0\times10^{-3}$
  for the five-stem vector) --- the redshift-space growth signal the isotropic
  monopole cannot see.
\item \textbf{The quadrupole matters more for ASTRA than for the 2PCF}
  ($3.6\times$ vs $2.4\times$): environment-dependent velocity fields give the
  quantile quadrupoles extra independent RSD information.
\item \textbf{The greedy poll confirms the hierarchy.}  Of the first six selected
  (piece, multipole) units, four are monopoles and two are quadrupoles; greedy
  front-loads the information-dense monopoles but reaches a quantile quadrupole
  (\code{dQ2}-$\ell2$) as early as step four, so certain quadrupoles carry strong
  \emph{independent} information.
\end{itemize}

The verdict is to use mono$+$quad: the monopole is primary, the quadrupole is
complementary (mostly $\sigma_8$ and $n_s$), and its value is larger in the
environment splits than in the plain 2PCF.  This refines the earlier impression
that ``the quadrupole adds almost nothing'' --- that held for $\omega_b$
specifically and under a Hartlap-limited bin budget; with the pooled covariance
the quadrupole clearly pays off.

for the full auto and the five-stem vector.  Right: greedy poll over the twelve

\section{Conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item The systematic sweep of all 16 singles and 120 pairs is dominated, at
  face value, by vectors with a random-quantile leg (gains up to $55\times$).
  The cause is specifically the random \textbf{quadrupole}: the
  environment-classified random \emph{monopoles} carry real cosmic-web signal,
  but the random quadrupoles have no peculiar-velocity (RSD) signal and their
  noisy derivatives inflate the Fisher.  Flagging the random legs is a
  conservative choice (it also sets aside the real random-monopole information);
  any ranking here must noise-correct the random quadrupole, e.g.\ by propagating
  the derivative variance, before trusting those gains.
\item Among the \textbf{trustworthy data legs}, the full$\times$data-quantile
  crosses at the density extremes (\code{xdQ1}, \code{xdQ4}) are the best
  single additions ($\FoM_3$ gain $\approx 5$), and the best clean pair is
  the void data auto with the void data cross, \code{dQ1}$+$\code{xdQ1}
  ($\FoM_3$ gain $14$, $\sim\!2.4\times$ per parameter on
  $\omega_b,\omega_c,n_s$).  The gains are pure degeneracy breaking: each
  vector is worse than the full auto alone.
\item A \textbf{greedy chain} over the data legs
  (\code{full}$+$\code{xdQ1}$+$\code{dQ1}$+$\code{dQ2}$+$\code{xdQ3}$+$\code{dQ4})
  reaches $\FoM_3 = 43\times$ at five stems, tightening the standard-2PCF errors
  by $3.4$--$4.1\times$ on $\omega_b,\omega_c,n_s$ and $2.8\times$ on $\sigma_8$
  (Fig.~\ref{fig:corner}).  Returns diminish sharply ($5.1\to2.7\to1.8\to1.3$
  per step), so $\sim\!4$ stems is the practical optimum, and the gains come from
  \emph{spanning} the four environments rather than repeating one.
\item Single vectors do not improve $\sigma_8$ (HOD-amplitude degeneracy,
  unreliable derivative), but the multi-environment combination does so modestly;
  $\FoM_3$ excluding $\sigma_8$ remains the trustworthy score.
\item \textbf{Use both multipoles.}  The monopole is the workhorse and the
  quadrupole is weak alone, but adding the quadrupole multiplies $\FoM_3$ by
  $2.4$--$3.6\times$ and is the dominant source of $\sigma_8$ information; its
  value is larger for the ASTRA splits than for the plain 2PCF
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:multipole}).
\item \textbf{Next steps.}  (i) Propagate the GP emulator derivative variance
  (calibrated with the $\sim\!1.25\times$ inflation measured separately) so the
  noisy random-quadrupole derivatives are deflated and the random legs can be
  ranked honestly --- this should recover the genuine random-monopole
  information; (ii) use the higher-iteration runs to denoise the
  random-quadrupole derivatives directly; (iii) revisit the four-parameter FoM once a
  trustworthy $\sigma_8$ derivative is available; (iv) estimate the covariance
  from many independent full boxes (e.g.\ the 25 AbacusSummit phases) before
  quoting absolute error bars --- the relative rankings here are robust to the
  $\sim\!6\%$ level (Sec.~\ref{sec:covrobust}), but the absolute $\sigma$ rely on
  the subbox approximation.
\end{enumerate}

All numbers are reproduced by \code{scripts/fisher\_vector\_search.py} (the full
sweep; ranked table in
\code{plots/vector\_search/vector\_search\_results.csv}),
\code{scripts/fisher\_greedy\_chain.py} (the greedy chain), and
\code{scripts/fisher\_compare\_full\_vs\_5stem.py} (the corner comparison), and
\code{scripts/fisher\_5stem\_details.py} (the step-by-step anatomy of
Sec.~\ref{sec:anatomy}), and \code{scripts/fisher\_multipole\_compare.py} (the
monopole/quadrupole study of Sec.~\ref{sec:multipole}).
